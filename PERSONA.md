# Prototype Persona: Daniel Mercer

Daniel Mercer is a fictional buyer used internally to keep Booth Marketing focused on a realistic B2B decision maker. He is not a real client, testimonial or case study.

## Profile
- Name: Daniel Mercer
- Age: 42
- Location: Manchester, UK
- Role: Managing Director
- Company: Mercer Commercial Services Ltd
- Business: B2B facilities and compliance services
- Team: 28 employees
- Annual revenue: approximately £2.8m
- Time in business: 11 years
- Typical clients: property managers, commercial landlords, offices, warehouses and regional businesses

## Existing tools
Daniel's company already uses normal business software:
- Outlook
- Excel
- HubSpot
- Xero
- Microsoft Teams
- SharePoint
- Website enquiry forms
- A job management system

His problem is not a lack of software. His staff manually connect the software together.

## Typical operational friction
- A new enquiry arrives and somebody manually adds it to HubSpot.
- The right salesperson needs to be told about the lead.
- Follow-up depends on somebody remembering the next step.
- Client information gets copied between email, spreadsheets and internal systems.
- New clients trigger the same emails, folders, documents and tasks every time.
- Management reports require staff to gather information manually.
- Certain processes slow down when a key employee is away because that person knows how the workflow works.

## Main frustrations
- Repetitive admin absorbs experienced employees' time.
- Important processes depend on individual memory.
- Information is duplicated across systems.
- Leads are followed up inconsistently.
- New clients create repeated onboarding work.
- Management reporting requires manual gathering.
- Growth creates more back-office work.
- Adding customers often means adding administrative effort at nearly the same rate.

## What Daniel does not want
- An AI experiment with no clear business case.
- A complete replacement of the software stack that already works.
- Technology that creates more complexity for the team.
- An agency that recommends AI simply because AI is fashionable.
- Impressive demos that do not translate into a reliable operating process.

## Questions the website must answer
- Could this process actually be automated?
- Can you work with the systems we already use?
- Will we need to replace our existing software?
- What would you automate first?
- Will the team understand the new workflow?
- What happens when automation cannot handle an exception?
- What should remain human?
- Do I need to know what I want built?
- Is this worth changing at all?
- Can this help us increase capacity without adding the same amount of admin overhead?
- What happens during the Automation Audit?
- Does requesting an audit commit me to a project?

## Buying trigger
Daniel contacts Booth Marketing when he believes:

> These people understand why running my company feels harder than it should, and they will examine the process before trying to sell me technology.

## Website implication
Every page should prioritise operational recognition over AI terminology. The website should show concrete manual processes, explain what a clearer flow could look like, reduce implementation anxiety and make the Automation Audit feel like a low-risk first step.
